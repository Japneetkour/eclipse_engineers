FINAL ANSWER
================================================================================================================
1. Yes - holding all 30 skills, experience and location fixed, the title still pays: relative to the average title, model A premiums run from +45.2% (ML Engineer) down to -23.0% (Other), and in model B ML Engineer still earns +30.9% (95% CI +21.9% to +40.4%, p = 7.9e-14) and Data Scientist +16.0% (95% CI +9.0% to +23.5%, p = 3.1e-06), with 6 of 7 title coefficients still significant at 5%.
2. Skills buy only part of that gap: the raw premium shrinks by 38% on average (31% for Data Scientist, 28% for ML Engineer) and R2 moves just 0.480 -> 0.493 (+0.013), even though the skill block is jointly significant (Wald chi2(30) = 463, p = 4.2e-79).
3. Verdict: titles mostly reflect real but only partly measurable differences rather than being empty labels - the Data Analyst and Data Scientist skill profiles are just 0.47 cosine-similar (python 31.7% vs 3.2%, machine learning 30.9% vs 2.1%), so the skills we can see are genuinely different and partly priced, yet 62% of every premium survives those controls (Manager even rises +0.3% -> +8.0%), which means titles pay for ability, seniority and brand that the 30 keyword skills do not capture.

LIMITATIONS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Observational data: title, skills and salary come from the same postings, so unmeasured ability/seniority/company mix can still drive both the title and the pay (selection, not causation) - and the 30 skills are keyword matches on a posting, not verified proficiency.
2. Salary is a band midpoint (0to3, 6to10, ...) rather than a real offer, location has 344 levels with 154 once-only cells (which forces HC1 instead of HC3), so SEs are wide for rare title x location cells, within-band pay variance is ignored, and small premiums (Data Engineer +3.6%) cannot be separated from zero.
